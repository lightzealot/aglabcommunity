What would really happen if you stole 10 billion Colombian pesos? My reel is a **work of fiction**, but the numbers and the rules are real. Here is every fact with its source, so you can see how the financial system works from the inside. And at the end, you decide what happens in part 2.

## Problem one: you can't even carry it

A bill weighs **about one gram**. That is an estimate: the Banco de la República does not publish the weight of its bills. The 100,000-peso bill measures 66 × 153 mm.

| How you got it | Bills | Approximate weight |
|---|---|---|
| 100,000-peso bills | 100,000 | about 100 kilos |
| 20,000-peso bills | 500,000 | close to half a ton |

## Problem two: the bank reports on its own

The UIAF (Colombia's financial intelligence unit) receives cash transaction reports from banks, whether anyone is suspicious or not:

| Type | Reported from |
|---|---|
| Single cash transaction | $10,000,000 pesos |
| Multiple transactions by the same client | $50,000,000 pesos per month |

It is automatic. Always.

## Problem three: splitting has a name

Depositing $9 million a day to stay just under the line is called **structuring** (fraccionamiento), and it is one of the first things banks are trained to spot.

| Day | Deposited |
|---|---|
| 1 | $9,000,000 |
| 2 | $18,000,000 total |
| 3 | $27,000,000 total |
| 4 | $36,000,000 total |

When the bank detects it, it files a **suspicious activity report (ROS)** with the UIAF, and that report is **confidential**: the bank cannot tell you.

## What the law says

Money laundering (Penal Code, art. 323) carries **10 to 30 years in prison** and a fine of 1,000 to 50,000 minimum wages.

One detail: in the story I said "structuring", not "crime". I found no standalone offense for structuring in Colombia. What is documented is the confidential ROS and the money laundering sentence.
